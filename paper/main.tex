\begin{abstract} 
We study an end-of-day liquidation problem in which a trader sells inventory through a central limit-order book before participating in a two-sided closing call. The continuous phase is constrained to one-sided liquidation, whereas the closing-call action set permits signed auction schedules and may therefore result in positive or negative terminal inventory. Whereas standard optimal {liquidation} models typically rely on terminal inventory penalties to manage end-of-day risk, ignoring the significant liquidity events available in closing auctions, we propose a deep reinforcement learning framework, consisting of a Deep Q-Network and its projected continuous-control actor–critic baselines (DDPG, TD3 and SAC), that explicitly incorporates this mechanism. We introduce an {optimal liquidation} framework designed to explicitly anticipate the closing auction, continuously refining the projected clearing price as the trading session evolves. We develop a generative stochastic market model to simulate the trading session and to emulate the market. We study the model in a synthetic rough-Heston setting and in a simulation driven by historical S\&P 500 mid-price paths, while order flow, auction updates and clearing remain generated by the simulator.
\vspace{3mm}

\noindent{\bf Key words:} {optimal liquidation}, auction trading, reinforcement learning, Deep Q-Learning
\vspace{3mm}

 
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Electronic Trading and Liquidation}


Market making is a cornerstone of modern electronic financial markets, providing liquidity by continuously posting buy and sell quotes while managing inventory and adverse selection risk with different participants having diverse objectives. Since the seminal work of Avellaneda and Stoikov \cite{avellaneda2008high} and its extension to explicit solutions in \cite{gueant2013dealing}, optimal market making has been studied extensively through stochastic control frameworks, leading to tractable strategies that balance expected profit against inventory risk under stylized assumptions on order flow dynamics and price evolution. We refer to \cite{cartea2015algorithmic} for a review of the literature on market making and high-frequency trading and to \cite{baldacci2021algorithmic,bellia2025market}  for recent advances in the topic. These models, however, typically rely on parametric assumptions that are difficult to validate empirically and may fail to adapt to the non-stationarity and strategic complexity of real-world markets.\vspace{0.5em}

The rapid growth of electronic trading and data availability combined with AI raising influence in financial industry has motivated the use of reinforcement learning as a flexible, data-driven alternative to classical control methods. Reinforcement learning allows a market maker to learn optimal quoting policies directly from interaction with the market, without requiring full knowledge of the underlying dynamics. Recent studies have demonstrated the promise of RL in market making and related problems, showing improved adaptability to complex market conditions, latent regimes, and evolving order flow patterns. The seminal article \cite{nevmyvaka2006reinforcement} introduces a Q-learning algorithm for market making problem on limit order book with many state variables.  It has been later extended in for example \cite{beysolow2019market,ning2021double,sirignano2019deep,gavsperov2021market,gueant2019deep,jerome2023mbt,leal2022learning} and adding regret analysis \cite{cesa2024market,cao2024logarithmic}. We also refer to \cite{hambly2023recent, cuchiero2024special,capponi2023machine} for comprehensive summaries of recent advances in RL techniques in finance.\vspace{0.5em}

While the notion of market making is an active topic of research including new developments with reinforcement learning techniques, one aspect remains insufficiently explored in the RL-based market making literature. At the end of the day, most exchanges are closing the market by triggering a closing auction, which plays a fundamental role in price discovery and liquidity provision in modern equity markets. Closing auctions concentrate a significant fraction of daily trading volume and often exhibit dynamics that differ markedly from those observed during continuous trading. Inventory held into the closing auction can be liquidated at a single clearing price, but doing so exposes the market maker to auction-specific price impact, imbalance risk, and strategic interactions. Traditional market making models typically ignore the auction or treat the end-of-day liquidation in an ad-hoc manner, while most reinforcement learning approaches focus exclusively on continuous limit order book trading.\vspace{0.5em}

We consider a trader who begins with a positive inventory and submits sell limit orders during a continuous CLOB phase. At the auction opening, the control set is enlarged to signed auction schedules. The agent learns a feature-based policy without knowing the transition law of the exogenous order flow, while the exchange clearing mechanism itself is specified by the model.

\subsection{On the Importance of (Closing) Auctions}

Early works in financial auctions has been developed in \cite{kyle1985continuous}. In this article, Kyle provides the first tractable equilibrium model of a continuous auction with asymmetric information.
It explains how prices aggregate private information through order flow, introducing measure of market depth and price impact. This paper became the benchmark for analyzing liquidity, price discovery, and strategic trading in modern auction-based financial markets. While auction markets have been well-investigated in the economical community for discrete-time model, see for example \cite{milgrom1989auctions,vulcano2002optimal,milgrom2004putting} and have known a growing interest, especially since the work of the Nobel laureate Paul Milgrom, it stays challenging at the high frequency-level and for continuous time framework and has been pointed as one of the most challenging question in financial engineering in \cite[Section 5.1]{carmona2022influence}. \vspace{0.5em}

The big picture of the trading session considered in this paper is the following. During continuous trading, the agent solves a one-sided end-of-day liquidation problem: she can only sell and her inventory remains nonnegative. During the closing call, the admissible action set is enlarged to permit two-sided auction schedules, so that terminal inventory may lie on either side of zero. The auction phase is therefore not constrained to monotone liquidation. Deviations from zero terminal inventory are discouraged through the terminal inventory penalty rather than prohibited by a hard position constraint. At the end of the auction phase, known as the clearing time, a clearing price is set by the exchange to ensure as much transaction as possible to clear the market and trade the asset. This closing auction plays a fundamental role in market liquidation and empowers efficiency of price discovery as explained in \cite{kandel2012effect,raillon2020growing}. We also refer to \cite{milgrom2019auction} for an overview of auction mechanism.\vspace{0.5em}

On the one hand, auctions successfully fix mechanical flaws of limit order book like correlation breakdown as explained in \cite{budish2014implementation}. On the other hand, unlike a LOB trading, the key challenge of the auction phase is to set an efficient (clearing) price to trade the asset, given the different operations of market participants, known as the price discovery mechanism \cite{madhavan2000price,biais2002ipo,biais1999price}. The benefit of auctions for market quality, as reducing the spread between the clearing price and the efficient price of a risk asset has been investigated in the recent literature, for example \cite{du2014welfare,paul2021optimal,derchu2024ahead,salek2024equity,jantschgi2024transaction} by proposing incentives and optimal fees scheme to mitigate auctions' flaws \cite{mastrolia2024clearing,mastrolia2025optimal}.\vspace{0.5em}

Finally, we see in many markets nowadays that volumes at the closing auction have increased significantly in recent years. Ignoring this stage is therefore problematic when modeling market making. Furthermore, high-frequency market makers aim at ending each trading day with a flat position: it is likely they will use the auction to close their position if they need to. This underlines the importance of considering closing auctions in an end-of-day market making or liquidation framework.

\subsection{Methodology, Contributions and Financial Insights}


This work proposes a new end-of-day liquidation and closing-call control model based on a reinforcement learning approach, designed to operate over a typical trading session while explicitly anticipating the closing auction at the end of the session. The goal of this exercise is to study whether different off-policy deep reinforcement learning methods are able to learn stable and effective end-of-day liquidation policies that anticipate a closing auction. We formulate a single-agent reinforcement-learning problem for one-sided CLOB liquidation with an explicitly modeled projected closing-call clearing signal. The proposed framework builds on deep reinforcement learning (DRL). We consider Deep Q-Network (DQN) for the discrete action formulation \cite{watkins1992q,mnih2013playing,fan2020theoretical} as the baseline, together with continuous-action actor–critic relaxations (DDPG, TD3, SAC) that lift the discrete quoting grid to a continuous one. These approaches have been successfully applied to a wide range of financial problems, including optimal asset allocation, optimal execution in dark pools, and market making; see, for instance, \cite{neuneier1997enhancing, ganesh2019reinforcement, kearns2013machine, ning2021double, baldacci2021algorithmic}. \vspace{0.5em}

More specifically, we compare a baseline DQN approach for liquidation with auction participation to a continuous-control extension. The goal of Q-learning is to find the optimal action-value function $Q$ which yields the approximately optimized feature-based policy. DQN consists in parameterizing the action-value function with a neural network $Q_\theta$ such that optimization over $Q$ becomes optimization over the weights $\theta\in \mathbb{R}^q$, for some $q \in \mathbb{N}^\star$. Because the trading session splits into a continuous (CLOB) phase and a closing-auction phase with structurally different state and action spaces, we train two phase-specific networks $Q_\phi$ and $Q_\psi$, coupled at the auction opening. The action-value function $Q_\theta$ is then trained by minimizing a temporal-difference error on minibatches drawn from an experience-replay memory $\mathcal D$ of fixed capacity $M$, which decorrelates consecutive transitions \cite{mnih2013playing}; training is further stabilized by a target network held fixed and refreshed periodically \cite{sutton1998reinforcement,mnih2015human} and by the double-$Q$ correction \cite{van2016deep}. To accommodate continuous controls and have more relevant means of comparisons to the DQN method, we additionally consider continuous-action actor-critic methods as a relaxation of the discrete formulation: DDPG \cite{lillicrap2020continuous}, TD3 \cite{fujimoto2018addressing} and SAC \cite{haarnoja2018soft}.\vspace{0.5em}

For comparison, we use a stylized reference benchmark adapted from the optimal market making model introduced by \cite{avellaneda2008high} and derived in \cite{gueant2013dealing}, which allows us to quantitatively assess the efficiency and performance of the Q-learning-based methods considered in this study. We furthermore compare the performance to the time-weighted average price strategy.\vspace{0.5em}

The structure of this work is the following. Section \ref{sec:marketmodel} describes the general trading session structure investigated. We first introduce the mathematical framework in Section \ref{sec:math}, providing rigorous definitions of all stochastic processes, agents, and market participants involved in the model. The continuous trading phase and the auction phase are respectively described in Sections \ref{sec:lob} and \ref{sec:auction}.
We then introduce the auction clearing mechanism in Section \ref{sec:clearing}, where we also state a well-posedness result for the stylized clearing equation: the existence of a clearing price under very general supply-demand functions given by Theorem \ref{th:clearing}. Section \ref{sec:proj} proposes an algorithm to predict a hypothetical clearing price during the limit order book trading session and to anticipate the conversion of unmatched limit orders into trade blocks for the future closing auction. At the phase boundary, a calibrated linear representation of the residual exogenous CLOB book is used to initialize the auction book. This algorithm lies at the core of our contribution, as it links the observed residual exogenous CLOB liquidity to the initial auction book. In our algorithm, the agent’s CLOB order is good for one interval and does not carry into the auction. Section 3 defines the controlled simulator configuration, the reduced feature map, admissible controls, the shaped training objective and the economic evaluation criteria. Section \ref{sec:learning}  introduces both the DQN algorithm and the continuous-control RL methods employed in our framework. Section \ref{sec:benchmark} recalls the main results of \cite{avellaneda2008high, gueant2013dealing}, which we use as a stylized reference benchmark to compare the profit-and-loss (PnL for short) performance of a market maker who anticipates the closing auction with one who does not. {Section \ref{sec:numerics} explains the numerical methods we considered in this work, that is a generative stochastic model of the market in  Section \ref{sub:generative}, and a stylized Heston model in Section \ref{sub:rough} used to generate synthetic data for the asset price, combined with limit order book parameters calibrated to reflect the projected future closing auction.} Finally, Section \ref{sub:historical} presents the numerical results obtained when using historical data of S\&P 500 assets for the mid price process.

\vspace{0.5em}
 
These numerical results highlight the benefits of anticipating the closing auction, as well as the effectiveness of the proposed deep reinforcement learning approach in maximizing the market maker’s PnL over a trading session for both stochastic rough models and historical data from the S\&P 500. 

\section{Market Model}\label{sec:marketmodel}

\subsection{Mathematical Framework and Trading Phases}\label{sec:math}

Along this work, we fix a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$, named the market, where $\Omega$ represents all the possible market configurations, $\mathcal F$ is a $\sigma$-algebra denoting the available information and $\mathbb P$ is the market probability. We consider a financial asset traded on the financial market along a trading session with price evolving randomly. We divide the trading period into two phases: a continuous phase on a limit order book with market makers and takers and an auction phase, seen as a usual closing auction. We fix 
 two deterministic times $0< \tau^\op< \tau^\cl$ representing respectively the opening and the closing of the auction phase and denote by $h= \tau^\cl- \tau^\op$ the auction duration. We assume that both $( \tau^\op,  \tau^\cl) \in \mathbb{N}^2$ and $h$ is a positive integer. Therefore, the trading horizon is divided into a continuous phase $[ 0,  \tau^\op )$, in which the trader interacts with the central limit-order book (CLOB) and a fixed length $h$ closing auction $[ \tau^\op, \tau^\cl]$. {The agent acts on a realized decision grid $0=t_0<\cdots<t_n<\tau^\op=t_{n+1} <\cdots<t_m<\tau^\cl=t_{m+1}$. During the continuous phase, the decision times $t_0,\ldots,t_n$ are random times generated by the exogenous market-order processes as in Definition \ref{def:timing}. Consequently, $n$ is random. The decision clock is nevertheless control-independent: conditional on the same realization of the exogenous market processes, the sequence $(t_i)_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n}$ is the same for every admissible policy. During the auction phase, decisions are made at deterministic one-unit intervals such that $t_{n+1+\ell}=\tau^\op+\ell$ for $\ell \in \llbracket 0,h \rrbracket$, where $t_{n+1+h}=\tau^\cl$ is terminal. In particular, $m=n+h$. The initial time $t_0$ is the start of the considered trading session, but need not be the actual opening time of the market.}\vspace{0.5em}
 
 We denote by $I_t$ the trader’s inventory at time $t$. This inventory is positive (resp. negative) for long position (resp. short positions) with respect to the traded risky asset.\vspace{0.5em}
 
We denote by $\alpha > 0$ the tick size of the asset, fixed by the exchange. We will consider three types of market participants in this study:
\begin{itemize}
\item a strategic trader, called the agent, who liquidates inventory through sell limit orders during continuous trading and submits signed schedules during the closing call,
\item exogenous market makers, fixing limit orders and providing liquidity during the continuous trading session on both sides of the LOB and proposing limit prices during the auction session,
\item exogenous market takers. These participants submit aggressive market orders during both the CLOB and the auction phases to buy or sell the asset.
\end{itemize}
While we will focus on the optimization of the agent along the day, we use the term exogenous to emphasize the fact that other market makers and takers' optimizations are not considered here. In fact, we consider a single-agent decision problem against fixed background policies. All ``exogenous'' market participants are represented by a fixed stochastic data-generating mechanism which will be introduced later. This does not mean these participants have no strategic motives in an actual market, but that their individual optimization problems and endogenous responses to the agent are outside the model. We are therefore solving a best-response problem against the specified background policy, not a market-equilibrium problem. The two trading phases are non-overlapping by construction. We view this as a stylized sequential, Xetra-like chronology rather than as a complete model of a US closing process in which auction order collection may overlap with continuous trading. In the historical-input experiments, US equity data determine only the mid-price paths; order flow, auction updates and clearing remain synthetic. We now turn to the details of the trading period $[0,\tau^\op)\cup[\tau^\op,\tau^\cl]$ composed by continuous trading activities on the CLOB followed by a closing auction.  

\subsubsection{Trading During the Continuous Phase \texorpdfstring{$[ 0,  \tau^\op )$}{}}\label{sec:lob}

During the continuous phase on the LOB, we assume that each market participant observes the mid price $S_t^\mathrm{mid} = \alpha k_t^\mathrm{mid}$ at any time $t< \tau^\op$, where $k^\mathrm{mid}_t\in \mathbb N$ represents the number of tick at which the mid price is priced. Exogenous market takers submit market orders on both side of the market and consume liquidity. The number of market takers arriving on the side $\zeta\in\{+,-\}$ follows a counting process denoted by $N^\zeta$, where $\zeta = +$ denotes the ask side, and $\zeta = -$ the bid side. In other words, $N_t^\zeta$ market takers have arrived on the side $\zeta$ (e.g., if $\zeta=+$, then $N^+$ corresponds to buying market takers, consuming liquidity on the sell side). We assume that each market taker $i \leqslant N_t^\zeta$ submits a market order with volume $\nu_t^{\zeta,i}$ on the $\zeta$ side of the CLOB at time $t$.\vspace{0.5em}

During the continuous phase, the agent acts as a one-sided liquidator and submits only sell limit orders. She submits limit orders characterized by a limit price denoted by $S_t^\bullet=\alpha k_t$ at time $t$ where $k_t$ denoted the number of tick chosen at time $t$ to price the asset, and a proposed volume $v_t$. The agent therefore submits an order characterized by the pair $(k_t,v_t)$ at time $t$ on the limit order book. We assume that the agent is selling her inventory $I$ on the LOB during the continuous phase. This order is thus a limit order on $v_{t} \leqslant I_{t}$ shares at price $S_{t}^\bullet = \alpha k_t$. 
\begin{remark}
    Note that $\delta_{t} :=k_t-k_t^\mathrm{mid}$ represents the number of tick between the sell limit order proposed by the agent and the mid price, seen as the ask-spread of the agent.
\end{remark}

The liquidity provided by exogenous market makers at price level $S_{t}^{\zeta,j} = \alpha k_{t}^{\zeta,j}$, where $k_{t}^{\zeta,j} = k_{t}^\mathrm{mid}+\zeta j$ for $j \in \mathbb{N}^\star$ and $\zeta \in \{+,-\}$ is given by the volume $V_{t}^{\zeta,j}$ at any time $t$. The depth of the order book on side $\zeta$ is given by
\[L_{t}^\zeta = \inf\{j \geqslant  1:V_{t}^{\zeta,j} =0\}.\]
We assume that there exists $L_\infty>0$ such that $V_t^{\zeta,j}=0$ for $j>L_\infty$.

\begin{assumption}
Conditional on the price level selected by the agent, her order has
priority over exogenous orders resting at the same price level.
\end{assumption}

To abstract from queue-position dynamics and latency competition, we adopted the favorable execution convention that the agent has execution priority with respect to other participants.

\begin{remark}
Based on the random arrivals of market takers, there is (at least partial) execution on the order of the agent if given that a market taker arrives at time $t$ the following condition is satisfied  
\[
   \sum_{i=N_{t-}^++1}^{N_t^+}\nu_t^{+,i} > \sum_{j =1}^{\delta_t-1} V_{t}^{+,j}.
\]
Note that as soon as the buying volume of market takers reaches selling index $k_t$ of the agent, her order gets (at least partially) executed.
\end{remark}

For $i\in\llbracket0,n\rrbracket$, let $E_{t_i}$ denote the total number of shares executed over $(t_i,t_{i+1}]$ from the order submitted by the agent at $t_i$. This quantity is observed at $t_{i+1}$ and is given by
\[\forall i \in \llbracket 0,n \rrbracket, \quad E_{t_i} = \max\left(0, \min\left(v_{t_i}, \sum_{j=N_{t_i}^++1}^{N_{t_{i+1}}^+}\nu_{t_{i+1}}^{+,j}- \sum_{j=1}^{{\delta_{t_i}-1}} V_{t_i}^{+,j}\right)\right).\]
We recall that $E_{t_i}$ is a random variable since $v_{t_i}$ and $V_{t_i}^{+,j}$ are random. The inventory of the agent between $t_i$ and $t_{i+1}$ is $I_{t_{i+1}} = I_{t_i}-E_{t_i}$.\vspace{0.5em}

Motivated by the reinforcement learning approach, we assume that the agent will trade during this session until the deterministic physical time $t_n=\tau^\op-1$ before the market switch to the closing auction phase, where $n$ is the final CLOB decision index; hence $t_0,\ldots,t_n$ comprise $n+1$ CLOB decisions.\vspace{0.5em}

Every order submitted by the agent at a CLOB decision time $t_i$ is good for the single decision interval $(t_i,t_{i+1}]$. Any unexecuted remainder expires without a cancellation fee at $t_{i+1}$. In particular, the order submitted at $t_n$ is active on $(t_n,\tau^\op]$ and any remaining quantity expires at $\tau^\op$. It is not transferred to the auction.

\subsubsection{Trading During the Auction Phase \texorpdfstring{$[\tau^\op, \tau^\cl)$}{}} \label{sec:auction} At time $\tau^\op$, the system transitions to an auction, opened by the exchange. Similarly to \cite{derchu2024ahead,mastrolia2024clearing} and motivated by the reinforcement learning approach with the Markov Decision Process modeling the agent interacting with the market, we assume that the agent is setting bids at deterministic fixed time along the auction duration.  

\begin{assumption}
    The agent bids along the auction at discrete times $\tau^\op=t_{n+1}<\dots<t_m<\tau^\cl$.
\end{assumption}
The inventory $I_{ \tau^\op}$ of the agent remaining from the continuous phase, \textit{i.e.} that has not been liquidated, is then traded on this auction. More precisely, for all $t \in \{t_{n+1},\dots,t_m\}$, the agent observes exogenous market and limit orders arriving on the auction. Market orders are composed by a certain volume to be bought/sold no matter the price is set at the clearing time by the exchange, while limit orders are set along the auction through a supply function (functional volume to sell/buy below/above a certain price). Every market participant can cancel prior orders, unlike in the continuous trading phase. The agent chooses an action, which will be a limit order to submit, and/or the cancellation of a previous order. In this sense, the agent reacts to the environment (since she posts her order after seeing the orders of other market participants). After her final action at the terminal trading time $t_m<\tau^\cl$, the system transitions to a final state that will allow to compute the clearing price and the exchanged volume, thus allows to compute the terminal reward of the agent. Exogenous market participants do not modify their offers from $t_m$ to $\tau^\cl$. Solely the agent can cancel his older orders and submit a final limit order. Similarly to \cite{du2014welfare,paul2021optimal,mastrolia2025optimal} we assume that the agent submits a linear supply curve to the auction stated in the following assumption.

\begin{remark}
The ordering of moves immediately before clearing is a maintained stylization of the model, as the agent has a final reaction opportunity. Numerical conclusions, particularly those concerning late cancellations, are conditional on this convention.
\end{remark}

\begin{assumption}
    The agent has a linear supply curve $\Sigma_t:p\in \mathbb{R}_+ \longmapsto  K_t^a(p-S_t^a)$ for all $t \geqslant  \tau^\op$, where $K_t^a\geqslant 0$ and $S_t^a\in \alpha\mathbb N$.
\end{assumption}


At each $t_j\in\{t_{n+1},\dots,t_m\}$ the agent controls $K_{t_j}^a \geqslant  0$ and $S_{t_j}^a\in\alpha\mathbb N$ so that $\Sigma_{t_j}(p)$ represents net supply, that is the number of shares the agent is willing to sell at price $p$. If $\Sigma_{t_j}(p)\leqslant 0$ the agent is willing to buy at price $p$ or below and conversely if $\Sigma_{t_j}(p)\geqslant  0$ the agent is willing to sell at price $p$ or above. Allowing both signs enlarges the auction control set beyond monotone liquidation. The objective nevertheless remains end-of-day inventory reduction, enforced through the terminal inventory penalty.\vspace{0.5em}

We allow the agent to cancel her past bids at cost $d_j = (j-n-1)d$ at time $t_j$ for $j \in \{n+1,\ldots, m\}$ and $d>0$. Let $c_{t_j} \in \{0,1\}$ where $c_{t_j} = 1$ if and only if, at time $t_j$, the agent cancels all orders submitted prior to time $t_j$. In particular, at the opening of the auction, we set $c_{t_{n+1}} = 0$. We track these actions through a variable $\theta_{t_j}$, with $\theta_{t_n} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\theta_{t_{n+1}} = \mathbf 0$, where $\mathbf 0\in \mathbb{R}^{h}$ is the $m-n$-vector with 0 components. We recursively define $\theta_{t_j} =  \theta_{t_{j-1}}\vee  c_{t_{j-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{j-n-2}\mathbf{e}_{k}$ for $j \in \llbracket n+2,m+1\rrbracket$ where $\mathbf{e}_{k} = (\delta_{i,k})_{1 \leqslant i \leqslant m-n}\in \mathbb{R}^{m-n}$. By definition, $\theta_{t_j}^{(s)}$ indicates whether the order submitted at time $t_{n+s}$ for $s \in \{1,\ldots,j-n-2\}$ has been canceled prior to time $t_j$.

\begin{remark}
    In this formulation, the cancellation penalty $d_jc_{t_j}$ scales linearly with the time index $j$. We interpret this cost not as a strictly cumulative fee on historical orders -- which formally could be subjected to redundant cancellations --  but rather as a structural mechanism by the exchange to penalize ``illusory liquidity'': it is an economic modeling device designed to discourage repeated provision and withdrawal of liquidity. The ``fictive'' auction reward introduced below is instead an algorithmic shaping term. It is not an exchange rebate and is not included in the economic PnL criterion.
\end{remark}

At each trading time $t\in \{t_{n+1},\dots, t_m\}$, the agent submits an order $(K_t^a, S_t^a, c_t)$ to the market. Note that with the definition above, $c_t$ has no impact on the order $(K_t^a,S_t^a)$, only on orders sent at time strictly before $t$. The inventory of the agent remains frozen during the auction, \textit{i.e.} $I_t = I_{ \tau^\op}$ for $t< \tau^\cl$.\vspace{0.5em}

During the auction, we suppose in addition that both exogenous market makers and market takers are present in the auction. At each trading time $t$, the number of bids sent by exogenous market makers is denoted by $M_t$. Each bid sent by these actors is a limit order, each with volume $g_{i,t}(p)$, which is the supply schedule of the $i$-th bid offer present at time $t$ for $i \leqslant M_t$. Each exogenous $g_{r,t}$ is a signed net-supply schedule: positive values represent supply and negative values represent demand. \vspace{0.5em}

Market takers submit market orders in the auction. Let $N_t^+$ (resp. $N_t^-$) be the number of buying (resp. selling) market orders arrived up to time $t\in \{t_{n+1},\dots,t_m\}$. For $\zeta \in \{+,-\}$, market taker $i \leqslant N_t^\zeta$ submits a volume $\nu_t^{\zeta,i}$. Market takers can cancel their order along the auction, for instance, volume $\nu_{t_{n+s}}^{\zeta,i}$ can be set to zero at a time $t_j$ should the market maker of the $i$-th bid on side $\zeta$ decide to cancel his order from time $t_{j-1}$ to $t_j$, with $i \leqslant N_{t_{j-1}}^\zeta$, for all $j \in \{n+1,\dots,m\}$ and $s \in \{1,\dots,j-1-n\}$. If no cancellation occurs, we keep $\nu_{t_{j-1}}^{\zeta,i} = \nu_{t_j}^{\zeta,i}$ for $i \leqslant N_{t_{j-1}}^\zeta$. New market  orders having arrived at time $t$ are thus indexed by $N_{t_{j-1}}^\zeta < i \leqslant N_{t_j}^\zeta$. Note that with this notation, there are indeed more and more market orders in the market over time, as the auction progresses.

\subsubsection{Clearing Price Rule and Estimation Along the Auction}\label{sec:clearing}

After time $t_m$, the system moves into a final stage to set the clearing price of the auction. The market makers and takers can first send a last order in the auction, then the agent can still send a final limit order and/or cancel past ones. At time $t = \tau^\cl$, the auction matches total demand and supply to maximize the exchanged volume at a uniform clearing price $S_{\tau^\cl}^\cl$. In our stylized continuous clearing mechanism, the uniform clearing price is defined as the zero of aggregate net supply and solves the equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hyp_cl_auction}
    \sum_{i = 1}^{M_{t_m}}g_{i,t_m}(p) + \sum_{s=n+1}^{m} \left(1-\theta_{t_{m+1}}^{(s-n)}\right)K_{t_s}^a(p-S_{t_s}^a)- \sum_{\zeta \in \{+,-\}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t_m}^\zeta} \zeta \nu_{t_m}^{\zeta,i}= 0,\text{ for } p\in \mathbb{R}_+.
\end{equation}

Along the auction, we assume that the agent computes the projected clearing price by solving the equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hyp_cl_auction_estimation}
    \sum_{i = 1}^{M_{t_{j-1}}}g_{i,t_{j-1}}(p) + \sum_{s=n+1}^{j-1} \left(1-\theta_{t_j}^{(s-n)}\right)K_{t_s}^a(p-S_{t_s}^a)- \sum_{\zeta \in \{+,-\}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t_{j-1}}^\zeta} \zeta \nu_{t_{j-1}}^{\zeta,i}= 0,\text{ for } p\in \mathbb{R}_+
\end{equation}
which is the clearing price equation were the auction to close at time $t_j \in \{t_{n+2},\ldots, t_{m+1}\}$. The estimation at time $t_j=t_{m+1}=\tau^\cl$ corresponds to solve \eqref{eq:hyp_cl_auction} fitting with the exchange clearing rule.

\begin{remark}
For $j\geqslant  n+2$, the label $H_{t_j}^\cl$ denotes the indicative price available to the agent at decision time $t_j$ obtained by solving \eqref{eq:hyp_cl_auction_estimation} at time $t_j$. It is computed from the exogenous auction book recorded at $t_{j-1}$ and from all still-active agent schedules submitted up to and including $t_{j-1}$. Consequently, the time index of $H_{t_j}^\cl$ records when the estimate becomes available, rather than the date of the underlying book snapshot.
\end{remark}

We now provide sufficient conditions on ensuring existence of a solution to \eqref{eq:hyp_cl_auction_estimation}. First, note that Equation \eqref{eq:hyp_cl_auction_estimation} can be written as $\Phi_j(p)=0$ where
\begin{align*}
    \Phi_j(p) = \sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_{j-1}}} g_{i,t_{j-1}}(p) + A_jp-B_j-C_j
\end{align*}
with
\[A_j = \sum_{s=n+1}^{j-1} \left(1-\theta_{t_j}^{(s-n)}\right)K_{t_s}^a, \quad B_j = \sum_{s=n+1}^{j-1} \left(1-\theta_{t_j}^{(s-n)}\right)K_{t_s}^aS_{t_s}^a, \quad C_j = \sum_{\zeta \in \{+,-\}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t_{j-1}}^\zeta} \zeta \nu_{t_{j-1}}^{\zeta,i}.\]

\begin{theorem}[Existence of a unique (estimated) clearing price]\label{th:clearing}
Let $t_j \in \{t_{n+2},\ldots,t_{m+1}\}$. Assume that $\lim_{p\to +\infty} g_{i,t_{j-1}}(p)=+\infty$
and $p\longmapsto g_{i,t_{j-1}}(p)$ is continuous and strictly increasing for any $i\leqslant M_{t_{j-1}}$. Assume moreover $\Phi_j(0)\leqslant 0$, and the nondegeneracy condition ``$M_{t_{j-1}}\geqslant 1$ or $A_j > 0$'' holds. Then, the estimated clearing price equation \eqref{eq:hyp_cl_auction_estimation} admits a unique solution. 
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Clearly, $\Phi_j$ is continuous, $\lim_{p\to +\infty} \Phi_j(p) = +\infty$ and for $q>p$,
\[\Phi_j(q)-\Phi_j(p) = \sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_{j-1}}} \left[g_{i,t_{j-1}}(q)-g_{i,t_{j-1}}(p)\right] + A_j(q-p), \]
where $A_j \geqslant 0$. If $M_{t_{j-1}}\geqslant 1$, the first term is strictly positive, and if $M_{t_{j-1}}=0$, then $A_j>0$ and the second term is strictly positive. Hence $\Phi_j$ is strictly increasing. The intermediate value theorem gives existence of a zero, and strict monotonicity gives uniqueness. Since $\Phi_j(0)\leqslant 0$, the zero is nonnegative.
\end{proof}

Theorem \ref{th:clearing} provides conditions under which the clearing equation in our model admits a unique solution when the background participants' supply and demand schedules are not restricted to be linear. In practice, market participants submit collections of limit orders which the exchange aggregates to reconstruct the supply/demand functions. We refer to \cite{mastrolia2025optimal} for an example. These supply/demand functions need not be linear, and in fact that is not even standard in general. Finally and as we have mentioned earlier, our clearing rule recovers the one stated in \cite{paul2021optimal} or \cite{mastrolia2025optimal} for linear supply and demand curve as stated in the following proposition. 
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:linear}
   Fix $j\in\{n+2,\ldots,m+1\}$. Assume that $g_{i,t}(p) = K_t^i(p-S_t^i)$ with $K_t^i \geqslant 0$, $\sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_{j-1}}} K_{t_{j-1}}^i +A_j >0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_{j-1}}} K_{t_{j-1}}^iS_{t_{j-1}}^i +B_j +C_j \geqslant 0$. Then there exists a unique clearing price solving \eqref{eq:hyp_cl_auction_estimation} and given by  
   \[p = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_{j-1}}} K_{t_{j-1}}^iS_{t_{j-1}}^i +B_j +C_j}{\sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_{j-1}}} K_{t_{j-1}}^i +A_j}\geqslant 0.\]
\end{proposition}

In what follows, we impose the conditions of Theorem \ref{th:clearing} at each auction evaluation time, so that the estimated and terminal clearing prices are well defined. Precisely:
\[D_j = \sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_{j-1}}} K_{t_{j-1}}^i +A_j > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad F_j = \sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_{j-1}}} K_{t_{j-1}}^iS_{t_{j-1}}^i +B_j +C_j \geqslant 0.\]

Note that since prices lie on a grid with tick $\alpha$, the continuous theoretical root of Equation \eqref{eq:hyp_cl_auction_estimation}  is rounded to the closest tick. More precisely, if its solution is $p_j^\star$, the numerical indicative price is $p_\alpha=\alpha \lfloor p_j^\star/\alpha +1/2\rfloor$. This creates a subsequent absolute aggregate imbalance of at most $D_j\alpha/2$, which we solve numerically with a stylized pro-rata allocation rule.\vspace{0.5em}

The agent’s requested signed quantity at the rounded clearing price
\[\tilde Z_{ \tau^\cl} = \sum_{s= n+1}^{m}\left(1-\theta_{t_{m+1}}^{(s-n)}\right)
K_{t_s}^a\left(S_{\tau^\cl}^\cl - S_{t_s}^a\right).\]
Let
\[\forall p \in \mathbb{R}_+,\quad \Sigma_{\tau^\cl}^a(p) = \sum_{s= n+1}^{m}\left(1-\theta_{t_{m+1}}^{(s-n)}\right)
K_{t_s}^a\left(p - S_{t_s}^a\right).\]
At the rounded terminal price $p_\alpha$, total
supply and demand are
\[
Q^S(p_\alpha) = \sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_m}}g_{i,t_m}(p_\alpha)_+ + \Sigma_{\tau^\cl}^a(p_\alpha)_+ + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{t_m}^-}\nu_{t_m}^{-,i},\]
and
\[
Q^D(p_\alpha) = \sum_{i=1}^{M_{t_m}}(-g_{i,t_m}(p_\alpha))_+ + (-\Sigma_{\tau^\cl}^a(p_\alpha))_+ + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{t_m}^+}\nu_{t_m}^{+,i}. \]
Let
\[ \rho_S = \left\{\begin{array}{ll} 1 & \text{if }Q^S=0\text{ or }Q^S\leq Q^D\\ Q^D/Q^S & \text{if }Q^S>Q^D \end{array}\right.\]
and
\[\rho_D = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1, & \text{if }Q^D=0\text{ or }Q^D\leq Q^S\\ Q^S/Q^D & \text{if }Q^D>Q^S \end{array}\right..\]
The actual executed volume of the agent at the clearing time is then given by \[Z_{\tau^\cl} = \rho_S(\tilde Z_{\tau^\cl})_+ - \rho_D(-\tilde Z_{\tau^\cl})_+.\]
The agent's active schedules are aggregated into a single net schedule before this allocation, so no self-trade is counted. A negative terminal inventory is an admissible outcome of the enlarged auction action set rather than a violation of an inventory constraint. Deviations from zero are discouraged through the terminal inventory penalty.

\subsubsection{Projected Hypothetical Clearing Price During the Continuous Session}\label{sec:proj}

During the continuous phase $[0,\tau^\op)$, we assume that the agent is estimating the clearing price of the auction. For that purpose, the agent observes all outstanding (unexecuted) limit orders and treats them as if they were submitted to a fictitious auction, where they would be jointly matched to infer the implied clearing price. The agent is then creating a projected hypothetical clearing price $H_{t}^\cl$ along the duration of the LOB trading before the closing auction starts. The computation is detailed in Algorithm \ref{alg:hyp_clearing_price}, the calibration of the different characteristics of the auction prices and parameters comes from \cite{paul2021optimal}.\vspace{0.5em}

At $t_i$, the previous strategic CLOB order has already expired. Therefore, $O_i$ denotes the standing exogenous CLOB multiset observed before the action at $t_i$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Computation of $H_{t_i}^\cl$ for $i \in \llbracket 0, n \rrbracket$}
\label{alg:hyp_clearing_price}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Tick size $\alpha>0$, smoothing parameter $\eta_H\in(0,1]$, initial value $H_0$
\STATE Initialize $H_0^\cl \gets H_0$
\FOR{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
\STATE Record standing orders before time $t_i$ by multiset $O_i \in \mathcal{M}_f (\alpha \mathbb{N}\times \mathbb{R}_+)$\COMMENT{$\mathcal{M}_f(\cdot)$ denotes the finite multisets.}
\IF{$O_i \neq \emptyset$}
\STATE $\mathcal{K}_i \gets \operatorname{proj}_1(O_i)/\alpha$ \COMMENT{Standing price levels}
\STATE Register $Q_i(k)= \sum_{(p,v)\in O_i} v\delta_{p,\alpha k}$ for $k \in \mathcal{K}_i$ and $Q_i(k)=0$ if $k\notin \mathcal{K}_i$
\FOR{$k\in \mathcal{K}_i$}
      \STATE $\hat e_i^k \gets \frac{1}{i} \sum_{s = 1}^i Q_s(k)$ \COMMENT{Average volume available at level $k$, with $\hat e_i^k>0$ for $k\in \mathcal{K}_i$}
      \STATE $\hat \varsigma_i^k \gets \frac{1}{i} \sum_{s = 1}^i Q_s(k)^2$ \COMMENT{Average squared volume available at level $k$}
      \STATE $\hat K_i^k \gets  \max(0,(2\hat e_i^k-\hat \varsigma_i^k/\hat e_i^k)\alpha^{-1})$ \COMMENT{Calibrated slope at level $k$}
\ENDFOR
\IF{$\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}_i} \hat{K}_i^k > 0$}
\STATE Solve $\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}_i}\hat{K}_i^k(\alpha k-p) = 0$ for $p$ and denote the solution $\tilde S_{t_i}$ \COMMENT{Clearing price rule}
\STATE $H_{t_i}^\cl \gets H_{t_{i-1}}^\cl + \eta_H(\tilde S_{t_i} - H_{t_{i-1}}^\cl)$ \COMMENT{Smoothed update rule}
\ELSE
\STATE $H_{t_i}^\cl \gets H_{t_{i-1}}^\cl$
\ENDIF
\ELSE
\STATE $H_{t_i}^\cl \gets H_{t_{i-1}}^\cl$
\ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Finally, the agent is penalized for submitting orders below the clearing price estimate $H_{t_i}^\cl$. This is because an order with a price below the hypothetical clearing price would tell the agent to rather wait for the auction to liquidate her shares. This penalty will be detailed in the next section as a penalization for the reward function.\vspace{0.5em}

Let $O_{\tau^\op-}^{\star}$ denote the multiset of exogenous CLOB orders remaining after all executions on $(t_n,\tau^\op]$. The unexecuted remainder of the agent's order submitted at $t_n$ expires at $\tau^\op$ and is excluded from this multiset. To map the residual CLOB book into the linear-schedule representation
used in the auction, we repeat the final calibration step of
Algorithm \ref{alg:hyp_clearing_price} with the residual multiset
$O_{\tau^\op-}^{\star}$ in place of the pre-action multiset $O_n$, while the earlier snapshots $O_1,\ldots,O_{n-1}$ are left unchanged. Denote the resulting slopes by
$\hat K_{\star}^k$ and let
\[\mathcal K_{\star} = \left\{ k: O_{\tau^\op-}^{\star} \text{ contains positive volume at $\alpha k$ and $\hat K_{\star}^k>0$}\right\}. \]
At the auction opening, we assume that the initial exogenous auction book contains
the carry-over schedules $g_{k,\tau^\op}^{\star}(p) = \hat K_{\star}^k(p-\alpha k)$ for $k\in\mathcal K_{\star}$. These schedules are included in $M_{\tau^\op}$ before any auction-specific arrivals are processed. Let $D^\star=\sum_{k\in\mathcal K^\star}\hat K_k^\star$ be the aggregate carry-over slope. If
$D^\star<D_\mu$, for some numerical lower slope bound $D_\mu >0$, we add the ``persistent'' fallback schedule $g^0(p) = (D_\mu-D^\star) \left(p-S_{\tau^\op}^{\mathrm{mid}}\right)$ which cannot be canceled. If
$D^\star\geqslant D_\mu$, one carry-over schedule with maximal
slope is designated as ``persistent''.

\begin{remark}
    This construction is a stylized linear representation of the residual exogenous CLOB book. It does not preserve the original side of each individual CLOB order. Instead, each retained price level becomes the zero of a two-sided linear net-supply schedule centered at that level.
\end{remark}

\section{Learning Problem for Optimal Liquidation with Closing Auction}\label{sec:MDP}

We now turn to the discretization of the problem.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:timing}
Set $t_0=0$. For every $i\geqslant0$ such that
$t_i<\tau^\op-1$ and every $\zeta\in\{+,-\}$, define $\tau_i^\zeta = \inf\{ s>t_i: N_s^\zeta>N_{t_i}^\zeta\}$ and $\tau_i:=\tau_i^+\vee\tau_i^-$ with the convention $\inf\emptyset=+\infty$. We set $t_{i+1} = (\tau^\op-1) \wedge \left[ \tau_i\vee(1+\lfloor t_i\rfloor) \right]$ until the bound $\tau^\op-1$ is attained. The final CLOB decision index is $n=\inf\{i\geqslant 0:t_i=\tau^\op-1\}$. Thus there are $n+1$ CLOB decisions, $t_0,\ldots,t_n$, and we set $t_{n+1}=\tau^\op$ and $t_{n+1+\ell}=\tau^\op+\ell$ for $\ell\in \llbracket 0,h \rrbracket$. Finally, $m=n+h$, so that $t_{m+1}=\tau^\cl$, and $\mathcal T=(t_i)_{0\leqslant  i\leqslant  m+1}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Except for the final capped interval ending at $\tau^\op-1$, the maximum $\tau_i^+\vee\tau_i^-$ means that a new decision occurs only after both sides have received at least one additional market order. For simulation, the realized grid is a functional of the exogenous order-flow path. At decision $i$, however, only $t_0,\ldots, t_i$ and information available at $t_i$ are revealed to the agent. The last CLOB decision occurs at the deterministic physical time $t_n = \tau^\op-1$, while the random index $n$ determines how many earlier CLOB decisions occurred.
\end{remark}

{In the following, $i$ denotes the decision index whereas $t_i$ denotes
the corresponding physical time. We do not identify these two quantities
during the continuous phase, since $(t_i)_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n}$ is random. Note however that during the auction phase the two are related explicitly by $t_{n+1+\ell}=\tau^\op+\ell$ for $\ell \in \llbracket 0,h \rrbracket$.
For simplicity, albeit an abuse of notation, when there is no ambiguity, we write a generic decision time as $t$.}\vspace{0.5em}

The latent state is denoted by $X$ and defined for any time $t\in \mathcal{T}$ as a tuple 
\[X_t = (t, X_t^1, X_t^2, X_t^3, X_t^4, X_t^5, X_t^6, X_t^7, X_t^8, X_t^9, X_t^{10}, X_t^{11}, X_t^{12}, X_t^{13}, X_t^{14}, X_t^{15}, X_t^{16}, X_t^{17}, X_t^{18}),\] where each attribute encodes one of the market characteristics before the choice of an action by the agent as detailed below.

\paragraph{State Variables}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inventory: $X_t^1 = I_t$ represents the inventory of the market maker at time $t$: per construction, we restrict $I_t \geqslant 0$ for $t< \tau^\op$ and $I_t$ frozen for $\tau^\op\leqslant t<\tau^\cl$ with $I_{\tau^\cl}\in \mathbb{R}$;
    \item Volume executed at the clearing: $X_t^2 =0$ for $t<\tau^{\cl}$ with $X_{\tau^{\cl}}^2=Z_{\tau^{\cl}}$ and $H_{\tau^\cl}^\cl = S_{\tau^\cl}^\cl$; 
    \item Hypothetical/estimated auction's clearing price: $X_t^3 = H_t^\cl$ represents the hypothetical clearing price as defined in Section \ref{sec:proj} during the continuous trading phase for $t<\tau^{\op}$ or the estimated clearing price as defined in Section \ref{sec:clearing} as the solution to Equation \eqref{eq:hyp_cl_auction_estimation} during the auction trading phase for $\tau^{\op}< t<\tau^{\cl}$ and $H_{\tau^\op}^\cl = H_{t_n}^\cl$;
    \item Limit order book depth: $X_t^4 = L_t^+ \mathbf{1}_{\{0 \leqslant t \leqslant \tau^\op-1\}}$ and $X_t^5 = L_t^- \mathbf{1}_{\{0 \leqslant t \leqslant \tau^\op-1\}}$ represent respectively the depth in the limit order book on the ask (resp. bid) side;
    \item Number of limit order in the auction: $X_t^6 = M_t\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant  \tau^\op\}}$;
    \item Number of investors in the auction: $X_t^7 = N_{t}^+ \mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant  \tau^\op\}}$ and $X_t^8 = N_{t}^-\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant  \tau^\op\}}$ represents respectively the number of aggressive order sent in the auction to buy (resp. sell) the asset;
    \item Cancellation history: $X_t^9 = \theta_t \mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant  \tau^\op\}}$ represent the vector of canceled orders in the auction up to time $t$;
    \item Mid price: $X_t^{10} = S_t^\mathrm{mid}$
    \item Volume sent by investors in the auction: $X_t^{11} = (\nu_{t}^{+,i}\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant  \tau^\op\}})_{1 \leqslant i \leqslant N_t^+}$ and $X_t^{12} = (\nu_{t}^{-,i}\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant  \tau^\op\}})_{1 \leqslant i \leqslant N_t^-}$ represent the number of aggressive orders sent in the auction to buy (resp. sell) the asset
    \item Volume in the limit order book (ask/bid side): $X_t^{13} = (V_t^{+,j}\mathbf{1}_{\{t \leqslant \tau^\op-1\}})_{1 \leqslant j \leqslant L_t^+}$ and $X_t^{14} = (V_t^{-,j}\mathbf{1}_{\{t \leqslant \tau^\op-1\}})_{1 \leqslant j \leqslant L_t^-}$ are the volume existing in the limit order book on the ask and bid side at any depth
    \item Limit order in the auction: $X_t^{15} = (g_{i,t})_{1 \leqslant i \leqslant M_t}$, with $X_t^{15} = \emptyset$ before the auction;
    \item Price history in the auction: $X_t^{16} = S^a(t)$ where $S^a(t):=(S_{t_{n+1}}^a, \ldots, S^a_{t-1},0,\ldots, 0)$ represents the vector of limit order prices submitted in the auction up to time $t$;
    \item Supply/Demand slope history: $X_t^{17} = K^a(t)$ where $K^a(t):=(K_{t_{n+1}}^a,\ldots, K_{t-1}^a,0,\ldots, 0)$ represents the slope of the limit order submitted up to time $t$;
    \item Decision index: $X_t^{18} = i$ if $t = t_i$ is the time of the $i$-th decision: let us denote $i_t$ the decision index of time $t$ for a generic time $t\in \mathcal{T}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:linear}
    In the linear specification of Proposition \ref{prop:linear} and in the numerical simulator, $g_{i,t}(p) = K_t^i(p-S_t^i)$ so $X_t^{15}$ can equivalently be represented by $((K_t^i, S_t^i))_{1\leqslant i \leqslant M_t}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Note that $X^{16},X^{17}$ are vectors of size $h=m-n$ with $0$ components after time $t$.
\end{remark}

The state vector $X_t$ is viewed as an element of the state space $\mathcal{X}$ induced by the above variables, that is
\[\mathcal{X} = [0,\tau^\cl]\times  \mathbb{R}^2\times \mathbb{R}_+ \times \mathbb{N}^5 \times \{0,1\}^h \times \alpha\mathbb{N}\times \Psi(\mathbb{R}_+)^4 \times \Psi(\mathcal{G}) \times  (\alpha \mathbb{N})^h\times \mathbb{R}_+^h\times \mathbb{N}\]
where
\[\mathcal{G} = \left\{ g \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_+):g \text{ is strictly increasing}, \lim_{p\to+\infty}g(p)=+\infty\right\}\]
is the space of functions that satisfy the assumptions of Theorem \ref{th:clearing}, and for a set $E$, the space $\Psi(E)$ is defined as
\[\Psi(E) = \bigsqcup_{k=0}^{+\infty} E^k\]
with $E^0 = \{\emptyset\}$.

\begin{remark}
    While exchanges like the NYSE usually only display statistics on the total imbalance between buy and sell orders combined with an indicative price, it does not display all the orders that have been submitted at each price.
\end{remark}

Theorem \ref{th:clearing} permits general exogenous schedules. From this point onward, the learning problem and the numerical simulator use the linear specialization of Proposition \ref{prop:linear} and Remark \ref{rem:linear}. Accordingly, the aggregate slope and intercept features used below are well defined. We assume that the simulator records the complete latent configuration $X_t$, while the learning algorithms receive only the reduced feature vector $\varphi(X_t)$. We assume that these aggregate features are available to the agent. This reduced state is $\varphi(X_t)$ with
\begin{align*}
    \varphi(X_t)&= (t,I_t, H_t^\cl,S_t^\mathrm{mid},i_t, L_t^+\mathbf{1}_{\{t < \tau^\op\}}, L_t^-\mathbf{1}_{\{t < \tau^\op\}}, V_t^{+,1}\mathbf{1}_{\{t < \tau^\op\}}, V_t^{-,1}\mathbf{1}_{\{t < \tau^\op\}},\\
    &\qquad M_t\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant \tau^\op\}}, N_t^+\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant \tau^\op\}}, N_t^-\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant \tau^\op\}}, C(X_t)\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant \tau^\op\}}, A_{i_t}\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant \tau^\op\}}, B_{i_t}\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant \tau^\op\}}, \\
    &\qquad G_t\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant \tau^\op\}}, \mathcal{I}_t \mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant \tau^\op\}}, J_t\mathbf{1}_{\{t \geqslant \tau^\op\}})
\end{align*}
with $A_{i_t}$ and $B_{i_t}$ given  by the expressions of $A_j$ and $B_j$ in Section \ref{sec:clearing}, $C(x) = \max_{1 \leqslant i \leqslant m-n}\{(1-x^9_i)\mathbf{1}_{\{x_i^{17}>0\}}\}$ for a state $x\in \mathcal{X}$, and
\[G_t = \sum_{i=1}^{M_t} K_t^i, \quad J_t = \sum_{i=1}^{M_t} K_t^i S_t^i, \quad \mathcal{I}_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N_t^+} \nu_t^{+,i}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_t^-} \nu_t^{-,i}.\]
Let $\mathcal{Y} = \varphi(\mathcal{X})$.

\paragraph{Action Variables} We now turn to the actions of the strategic market maker. Given a state vector $X_t\in \mathcal X$, we define the action vector $A_t$ as $A_t = (A_t^1, A_t^2, A_t^3, A_t^4, A_t^5)$ where each component represents a particular action.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Volume set in the limit order: $A_t^1 = v_t \mathbf{1}_{\{0 \leqslant t \leqslant \tau^\op-1\}}$;
    \item Depth offset in the limit order book: $A_t^2 =\delta_t\mathbf{1}_{\{0 \leqslant t \leqslant \tau^\op-1\}}$;
    \item Supply/demand slope and reference price: $A_t^3 =K_t^a\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau^\op \leqslant t \leqslant \tau^\cl-1\}}$;
   and $A_t^4 = b_t^a\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau^\op \leqslant t \leqslant \tau^\cl-1\}}$ such that $S_t^a = S_{\tau^\op}^\mathrm{mid}+\alpha b_t^a$;
    \item Order cancellation in the auction: $A_t^5 =c_t\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau^\op \leqslant t \leqslant \tau^\cl-1\}}$.
\end{itemize}
We view such actions as part of the ambient action set
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal A =
\llbracket0,V_{\max}\rrbracket \times \llbracket0,L_{\max}\rrbracket \times \beta \llbracket0,K_{\max}\rrbracket \times \llbracket-B_{\max},B_{\max}\rrbracket \times \{0,1\}
\end{aligned}
\]
where $V_\text{max}, L_\text{max}, B_\text{max}, K_\text{max} \in \mathbb{N}^\star$ are numerical bounds defining the finite action grid. In particular, $B_\text{max}$ is a numerical truncation parameter chosen sufficiently large for the simulated price range.\vspace{0.5em}

Recalling that on the limit order book the market maker is liquidating his inventory, hence submits a volume $A_t^1 \leqslant I_t = X_t^1$, at a price $S_t^\mathrm{mid}+\alpha A_t^2 \geqslant S_t^\mathrm{mid}$. During the auction phase, the market makers can cancel previous orders exactly once, thus $A_t^5 = c_t \leqslant \max_{1\leqslant i \leqslant t-\tau^\op}(1-\theta_t^{(i)}) = :C_t$ with the convention that $\max_\emptyset = 0$ (for $t\leqslant \tau^\op$).
\begin{definition}[Admissible actions]
    Given an observed state $y \in \mathcal{Y}$, the set $\operatorname{Adm}(y)$ of admissible actions is defined as
    \begin{align*}
        \operatorname{Adm}(y) &= \{a\in \mathcal{A}: a_1 \leqslant y_2, a_5  \leqslant y_{13}, y_4 +\alpha a_4 \geqslant 0,a_1=0\implies a_2 = 0, \\
        &\qquad a_3=0\implies a_4 = 0, (a_1,a_2)\mathbf{1}_{\{y_1\geqslant\tau^\op\}}=0, (a_3,a_4,a_5)\mathbf{1}_{\{y_1<\tau^\op\}} = 0\}
    \end{align*}
    where the last two components embed the phase compatibility of admissible actions.
\end{definition}


\begin{remark}
    Note that $S_t^\mathrm{mid}$ is constant during the auction. Furthermore, since all $X_t^1$, $X_t^{10}$ and $C(X_t)$ are encoded in $\varphi(X_t)$, all admissible actions for $X_t$ are exactly those admissible for $\varphi(X_t)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Admissible policies]
    An admissible policy is a measurable map \[\pi\colon y\in \mathcal{Y} \longmapsto \pi(\cdot \mid y) \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})\]
    such that $\pi(\operatorname{Adm}(y)\mid y)=1$ for all $y \in \mathcal{Y}$. We denote $\Pi$ the set of these admissible policies.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
     We use the word configuration for $X_t$ and feature vector for $\varphi(X_t)$. Neither process is claimed to be a finite-dimensional Markov state. The algorithms are feature-based numerical approximations for the specified controlled simulator, and no global optimality claim is made.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Reward} We define the reward on three regions as explained below.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item During the continuous trading session for $t<\tau^{\op}$. The market maker submit a price $S_t^\bullet=S_t^\text{mid}+\alpha A_t^2$. The volume executed is given by $E_t$. The cash proceeds are thus given by $S_t^\bullet\times E_t$. We moreover assume that the market maker penalizes the execution by comparing the price executed with the hypothetical clearing price $H_t^{\cl}=X_t^3$. If $S_t^\bullet>H_t^{\cl}$, the market maker receives the full profit otherwise if $S_t^\bullet<H_t^{\cl}$ the market maker may regret the execution. We assume that the difference between $X^3$ and $S^\bullet$ tolerated is given by $k^\star\alpha$ for some $k^\star$ fixed. It means that as soon as 
    \[(H_t^{\cl}-S_t^\bullet)_+\leqslant k^\star\alpha,\]
the market maker still get a profit from the execution on the limit order. We thus introduce a penalty function $f^c:\mathbb R\longrightarrow [0,1]$ continuous and increasing such that $f^c$ is zero on $\mathbb{R}_-$ and $f^c(k^\star\alpha)=1$, such that the reward of the market maker is given by 
\[r_t(X_t,A_t)= S_t^\bullet E_t f^c(k^\star \alpha -( H_t^\cl -S_t^\bullet)_+).\]
\item During the auction trading session for $\tau^{\op}\leqslant t<\tau^{\cl}$. The market maker submits a slope $K_t^a = A_t^3$ and a price $S_t^a = S_{\tau^\op}^\mathrm{mid}+\alpha A_t^4$. The agent receives a ``fictive'' reward $K_t^aH_t^\cl(H_t^\cl-S_t^a)$, where $H_t^\cl$ is the anticipated clearing price (were the auction to close at time $t$). The agent is penalized for canceling previous orders at cost $d_t$ at time $t$, yielding a penalty $-d_tc_t$. Here, $d_t$ is an exchange cancellation fee. Because the auction action set is two-sided, a submitted schedule may imply a projected purchase at the indicative price. This happens when $H_t^\cl \leqslant S_t^a$: the market maker is willing to buy $K_t^a(S_t^a-H_t^\cl)$ shares at price $H_t^\cl$ or below. To retain a liquidation preference in the training signal, we optionally attenuate the negative shaping term associated with such projected purchases. We hence introduce a function $f^a:\mathbb R\longrightarrow \mathbb R_+$ convex, continuous and decreasing such that $f^a$ is zero on $\mathbb{R}_+$ and $f^a(x) \leqslant (-x)_+$. The term $f^a(K_t^a H_t^\cl(H_t^\cl-S_t^a))$ attenuates or even removes the adverse consequence of buying. This attenuation is neither an admissibility constraint nor an economic cash flow. The reward of the market maker is given by
\[r_t(X_t,A_t) = K_t^a H_t^\cl (H_t^\cl-S_t^a) + f^a(K_t^a H_t^\cl(H_t^\cl-S_t^a)) - d_tc_t.\]
The ``fictive'' reward during the auction is a training-shaping term designed to provide a denser learning signal for actions that improve the agent's position relative to the current indicative clearing price. We consider here that the fictive reward is not restored in hindsight after cancellation of an order. Moreover, to retain a liquidation preference while permitting two-sided auction actions, we optionally attenuate the shaping reward associated with projected purchases. This term is a modeling choice in the training signal and is neither an admissibility constraint nor an economic cash flow.
\item Final reward at the clearing for $t=\tau^{\cl}$. At the clearing time, the clearing price $S_{\tau^\cl}^\cl$ is determined and orders are matched. The market maker makes the profit or loss $S_{\tau^\cl}^\cl Z_{\tau^\cl}$ based on the orders she sent to the market and did not cancel by the clearing time. The agent is furthermore penalized for holding inventory. We introduce $\lambda > 0$ as a penalization parameter. Furthermore, the agent's reward associated with purchases is once again attenuated. Since the midprice is frozen throughout the auction phase in our model, any inventory remaining after the auction is priced at the last exogenous midprice $S^\text{mid}_{\tau^\op}$ rather than at the auction clearing price. This separates the valuation of residual inventory from the endogenous auction price, which may be affected by the agent's own submitted schedules. The final reward of the market maker is given by
\begin{align*}
    G(X_{\tau^\cl}) &= S_{\tau^\cl}^\cl Z_{\tau^\cl}- \lambda |I_{\tau^\cl}|^2 + f^a\left (S_{\tau^\cl}^\cl Z_{\tau^\cl}\right) + S^\text{mid}_{\tau^\op}I_{\tau^\cl}.
\end{align*}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}
    In the numerical part we will choose $f^c (t) = \min(1,\frac{t_+}{k^\star \alpha})$ and $f^a(t) = q(-t)_+$ for some $q\in[0,1]$. One can interpret the penalty as removing a fraction $q$ of the reward. With $q = 1$, the purchase-side component is neutralized.
\end{remark}

To summarize, at time $t<\tau^\cl$ the random one-step reward is
\[r_t(X_t,A_t)=
\left\{\begin{array}{ll}
\displaystyle
S_t^\bullet E_t f^c(k^\star \alpha -( H_t^\cl -S_t^\bullet)_+), & 
\text{if } t< \tau^\op,\\
\displaystyle K_t^a H_t^\cl (H_t^\cl-S_t^a) + f^a(K_t^a H_t^\cl(H_t^\cl-S_t^a)) - d_tc_t, & \text{if }
 \tau^\op\leqslant t< \tau^\cl.
\end{array}\right.
\]

In our setting, the agent chooses action $A_t$ in state $X_t$. Then, the executed volume $E_t$ is realized, determining the one-step reward $r_t(X_t,A_t)$ during the state transition. Finally, the agent transitions into state $X_{t+1}$.\vspace{0.5em}

The learning objective of the agent is to solve
\begin{align*}
    (\mathbf{P})\quad \sup_{\pi \in \Pi} \quad & J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left [\sum_{i=0}^{m} r_{t_i}(X_{t_i},A_{t_i}) + G(X_{\tau^\cl}) \right] \nonumber \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \left \{\begin{array}{l}
    X_0 \sim \mu_0  \\
    A_{t_i} \sim \pi(\cdot \mid \varphi(X_{t_i}))
\end{array}\right..
\end{align*}

For comparison of policies, we consider the expected economic performance $\bar{J}(\pi)$, which corresponds to the expected marked-to-market PnL, and is defined as
\[\forall \pi \in \Pi,\quad \bar{J}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n} S_{t_i}^\bullet E_{t_i}+S_{\tau^\cl}^\cl Z_{\tau^\cl} +S_{\tau^\op}^\text{mid}I_{\tau^\cl}-S_0^\text{mid}I_0-\sum_{i=n+1}^{m} d_{t_i}c_{t_i}.\right]\]
We similarly define $\bar{J}_\lambda(\pi) = \bar{J}(\pi) - \lambda \mathbb{E}_\pi[I^2_{\tau^\cl}]$ the risk-adjusted expected marked-to-market PnL, for $\pi \in \Pi$. Without any reward shaping, we notice that $J(\pi) = \bar{J}_\lambda(\pi) + S_0^\text{mid}I_0$. The unshaped training criterion and the reported risk-adjusted economic criterion differ only by a constant, which unifies the base training objective with the economic objective. For precision, by the unshaped specification, we mean that the CLOB multiplier is set identically equal to one, the entire interim auction term $K_t^aH_t^\cl(H_t^\cl-S_t^a) + f^a\left(K_t^aH_t^\cl(H_t^\cl-S_t^a)\right)$ is omitted, and the terminal $f^a$ term is set to zero.

\section{Learning Algorithms}
\label{sec:learning}

In this section, we explain the numerical methods used to approximately solve $(\mathbf{P})$.

\subsection{Deep Q-Network}
\label{subsec:dqn}

We remind that $\mathcal Y=\varphi(\mathcal X)$ is the feature space used by the
learning algorithms. We consider the online episodic RL setting. In this setting, the agent executes the process sequentially for $E$ episodes and we denote the total number of episode samples $N_s = \sum_{e=1}^E(m_e+1)$. We consider an episodic, undiscounted, finite-horizon control problem. The transition law and conditional expected rewards of the simulator are not assumed known. We will therefore use a model-free method to train an approximately optimized feature-based policy $\hat\pi$ maximizing $J$. We approximate the action-value function by a neural network $Q_\theta(y,a)$ for some weight $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^q$, where $q \geqslant  1$. Our setting is however organized in two phases. For $y\in \mathcal{Y}$, let $t(y)$ be the time coordinate of $y$. We hence define a separate neural network for each phase:
\[\forall (y,a)\in \mathcal{Y}\times \mathcal{A},\quad Q_{\theta}(y,a) = Q_\phi(y,a) \mathbf{1}_{\{t(y)<\tau^\op\}}+Q_\psi(y,a) \mathbf{1}_{\{t(y)\geqslant \tau^\op\}}\] where $\theta = (\phi,\psi)$ and $\phi\in \mathbb{R}^{q_1},\psi \in \mathbb{R}^{q_2}$ and $q_1 + q_2 = q$. The terminal Q-function is given by the reward $G(X_{\tau^\cl})$ and we do not need to define a neural network. We train by Q-learning with experience replay and target networks (DQN, \cite{mnih2015human}) using the Double-DQN target of \cite{van2016deep}, and ensuring junction at $t=\tau^\op$ when the phase switch occurs. Note that the $\varepsilon$-greedy training policy is stochastic, while the reported greedy evaluation policy is deterministic after applying a fixed tie-breaking rule.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]

        \eta_i

\subsection{Continuous-Action Control}

The Deep Q-Network approach described above is adapted to the discrete action space of $(\mathbf{P})$. Discrete actions are naturally adapted for variables like a price, who is submitted according to a tick size anyway. For other variables however, like the supply/demand slope during the auction, it may be more effective to allow continuous actions. In this work, DQN is viewed as the discrete-RL baseline method and we want to compare it to different continuous-action methods. We hence consider a continuous relaxation of the action space. For each state $y \in \mathcal{Y}$, we introduce a relaxed continuous box of actions $\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{rel}}(y)\supseteq \mathrm{Adm}(y)$. Before a relaxed action is ``sent'' to the environment, it is transformed into an admissible market making action through a projection $\Gamma_y: \mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{rel}}(y) \longrightarrow \mathrm{Adm}(y)$. Informally, for $y \in \mathcal{Y}$ and an action $u\in \mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{rel}}(y)$, the environment executes $a= \Gamma_y(u)$, the replay buffer stores $u$ and the critic is trained on $u$. The precise componentwise projection $\Gamma_y$ used in the numerical implementation is provided with the accompanying code. The continuous-control algorithms interact with the same constrained market making environment as DQN, but they optimize over a continuous parametrization of the action. The environment executes $\Gamma_y(u)$, while the critic is parameterized on the raw action $u$. Because $\Gamma_y$ includes clipping, rounding and a binary cancellation threshold, it is generally nonsmooth and many-to-one. We therefore do not invoke the standard smooth-action convergence interpretation of these algorithms. \vspace{0.5em}

We consider three off-policy actor-critic algorithms: Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG), Twin Delayed DDPG (TD3), and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), see respectively \cite{lillicrap2020continuous}, \cite{fujimoto2018addressing}, and \cite{haarnoja2018soft}. DDPG uses a deterministic actor with exploration noise. TD3 adds twin critics, target-policy smoothing, and delayed actor updates. Finally, SAC uses a stochastic actor and entropy regularization. These methods are natural continuous-control counterparts to Q-learning and they all learn both the critic $Q_\theta$ and an actor $\mu_\omega \colon y\in \mathcal{Y}\longmapsto \mu_\omega(y)\in \mathcal A^{\mathrm{rel}}(y)$ with weights $\omega \in \mathbb{R}^l$ with $l\geqslant 1$ (such that $\Gamma_y(\mu_\omega(y)) \in \mathrm{Adm}(y)$). Note that SAC's actor is stochastic, that is $\pi_\omega(\cdot\mid y) \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal A^{\mathrm{rel}}(y))$, and not a point map. The main difference with DQN is that the maximization over actions in the Bellman target is no longer computed by enumerating all admissible actions. Instead, the next action is produced by a learned policy. We refer to \cite{achiam2018spinningup} for details on each of DDPG, TD3 and SAC and summarized in the following parameter table used for all numerical simulations. Each actor–critic method uses separate phase-specific actors, critics, target networks and replay buffers, and uses the auction critic for the continuation target at the phase boundary.

\section{Stylized Reference Benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmark}

We compare the performance of our DQN-learned policy and the continuous-control extensions against two different stylized reference benchmarks for optimal market making. Both benchmarks serve an illustrative purpose of closed-form approaches as we primarily compare the DQN policy against the continuous-control extensions. The first benchmark is adapted from the optimal market making models of Avellaneda and Stoikov \cite{avellaneda2008high} later solved explicitly by Guéant, Lehalle and Fernandez-Tapia \cite{gueant2013dealing}. We adapt the market making model to liquidation only and without risk aversion for the continuous phase. We then suggest an approximation allowing a straightforward application to our discrete-time setup. The second benchmark is the time weighted average price policy for the continuous phase. We adopt the same heuristic liquidation rule for the auction phase. These benchmarks are external reference policies for optimal liquidation and are not required to belong to the learned policy class $\Pi$. A theoretical solution to the optimal market making problem on a closing auction is left for future work.

\subsection{Avellaneda-Stoikov Optimal Market Making}\label{section:AS}


In this section, we will recall the main results of \cite{avellaneda2008high} and \cite{gueant2013dealing}, in the case where the market maker acts as a seller only. We consider the continuous phase, that is $t < \tau^\op$ in what follows. Suppose we consider a continuous time setup, that is we work on $[0,t_n]$, where $t_n$ is the last time of the continuous phase in our initial framework. The market mid price is assumed to follow an arithmetic Brownian motion $\mathrm{d}S_t^\mathrm{mid}=\sigma \mathrm{d}B_t$ with $\sigma>0$. We assume
that transactions have constant size $\Delta$. For simplicity, assume $\Delta = 1$. The inventory process writes $q_t = I_0 - N_t^a$, where $N^a$ is the point process, independent of $B$, giving the cumulative number of shares sold by the market maker. Formulated initially by Avellaneda and Stoikov, we assume that the intensity of $N^a$ depends on the spread $\delta_t^a = k_t^\bullet - k_t^\mathrm{mid}$ as $\lambda^a(\delta^a) = Ae^{-\alpha k\delta^a}$ for $A,k > 0$. The cash process $X_t$ of the market maker evolves according to $\mathrm{d}X_t = (S_t^\mathrm{mid}+\alpha\delta_t^a)\mathrm{d}N_t^a$. Let $T = t_n$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$ be the set of bounded predictable processes. The market maker optimizes
\[(\mathbf{M})\quad \sup_{\delta^a \in \tilde{\mathcal A}} \mathbb{E}\left [X_T + q_{T}S_{T}^\mathrm{mid} \right]\]

\begin{proposition}

The optimal quotes solving $(\mathbf{M})$ are given by
\[\delta^{a,\star}(t,q) = \frac{1}{\alpha k}\left [ 1+\ln \left ( \frac{v_q(t)}{v_{q-1}(t)}\right)\right],\] where 
\[\forall q \in \llbracket 0,Q \rrbracket, \quad v_q(t) = \sum_{j=0}^q \frac{(Ae^{-1}(T-t))^j}{j!}.\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Note that $(\mathbf{M})$ corresponds to the risk-neutral market maker $\gamma\to 0$ in the standard optimal market making problem investigated in \cite{avellaneda2008high,gueant2013dealing}. In this case, one proves that the problem is reduced to find the solution to the ODE system 
\[\forall q \in \llbracket 1,Q \rrbracket, \quad v_q'(t) = -Ae^{-1} v_{q-1}(t),\] and we directly get optimal quotes from \cite{gueant2013dealing} when $\gamma\to 0$ by \[\delta^{a,\star}(t,q) = \frac{1}{\alpha k}\left [ 1+\ln \left ( \frac{v_q(t)}{v_{q-1}(t)}\right)\right].\]

    We prove the result by induction. Note that $v_0 = 1$. Assume now that 
    \[\forall q\geqslant 1, \quad v_{q-1}(t)= \sum_{j=0}^{q-1} \frac{(Ae^{-1}(T-t))^j}{j!}.\] 
    We compute
\begin{align*}
    v_q(t) &= v_q(T)- \int_t^T v_q'(s)\mathrm{d}s =1 + Ae^{-1}\int_t^T v_{q-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s= 1 + Ae^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1} \int_t^T \frac{(Ae^{-1}(T-s))^j}{j!}\mathrm{d}s\\
    &= 1 + \sum_{j=0}^{q-1} \frac{(Ae^{-1})^{j+1}}{j!}\int_0^{T-t} s^j \mathrm{d}s= 1+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1} \frac{(Ae^{-1}(T-t))^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}= \sum_{j=0}^q \frac{(Ae^{-1}(T-t))^j}{j!}.
\end{align*}
This completes the proof by induction.
\end{proof}

The optimal quotes $\delta^{a,\star}(t,q)$ derived above hold in continuous time. These quotes yield, at time $t$ and given the current inventory $q$, the optimal quote price (that is $S_t^\mathrm{mid} + \alpha \delta^{a,\star}(t,q)$). However, we work in discrete time in our setting. Therefore, if at the end of period $t$, when action $(v_t,\delta_t)$ has been submitted, $m_t$ shares have been sold, it can be viewed as if on $[t,t+1)$, $m_t/\Delta$ fills occurred, all at price $\delta^{a,\star}(t,q)$. The volume to be submitted is $v_t = q_t$ in this approximation. This allows to ensure that if enough market takers come to the market, execution is not limited by the volume exposed by the market taker. The approximation is twofold: each of the fills is at price $\delta^{a,\star}(t,q_t)$ (instead of $\delta^{a,\star}(s,q_s)$ for $t\leqslant s < t+1$) and we expose the whole current inventory at any time $t$. The notion of ``exposed volume'' does not exist in the continuous time setting because of the fixed transaction size. To conclude, the action of the market maker on the continuous phase writes $(V_\text{max}\wedge \lfloor q_t\rfloor,\delta^{a,\star}(t,1\vee \lceil q_t\rceil))$ such that it is admissible, where we implicitly project $\delta^{a,\star}(t,1\vee \lceil q_t\rceil)$ itself on the set of admissible actions. 

\begin{remark}
    Note that whenever $\delta^{a,\star}(t,q_t)$ is not integer, we take the closest integer value instead.
\end{remark}

Once the auction opens, an inventory $q_{\tau^\op}\geqslant  0$ remains. We implement the following heuristic policy. Let $\tilde S$ be the average between the mean and the max price of executed orders during the continuous phase. If no CLOB execution occurred, we set $\tilde S:=H_{t_n}^\cl.$ The whole remaining inventory $q_{\tau^\op}$ is put on the auction at $\tilde{S}$, with supply function $\tilde{g}_z(p) = \min(q_{\tau^\op}, \min(zq_{\tau^\op}, \beta K_\text{max})(p-\tilde{S})_+)$. In Section \ref{sec:numerics}, we consider $z=10$. This single order is submitted right at the beginning of the auction, and only potentially executed at the clearing time. If $q_t=0$, the benchmark submits no CLOB order; the quote formula is used only for $q_t\geqslant 1$.

\subsection{Time-Weighted Average Price Benchmark}\label{section:TWAP}

The second benchmark is deliberately simpler. Given the current inventory $q_t$, the trader submits a deterministic volume
$v_t = V_\text{max}\wedge \lfloor q_t\rfloor \wedge  \lceil q_t/(T-t+1)\rceil$, quoted at the best ask price, \textit{i.e.} $\delta_t = 1$. This strategy corresponds to a uniform liquidation of the remaining inventory over the residual trading horizon. However, such a policy does not guarantee full liquidation during the continuous trading phase, as execution is conditional on order matching. For the auction phase, we adopt exactly the same liquidation policy as in the Avellaneda–Stoikov benchmark. 

\section{Numerical Simulations}
\label{sec:numerics}

This section employs the generative stochastic market model formulated in Section \ref{sec:marketmodel} to simulate continuous trading and closing auctions. We compare the DQN-learned policy against the continuous-control methods and the two stylized reference benchmarks: Avellaneda-Stoikov (AS) strategy, see Section \ref{section:AS} and the TWAP strategy see Section \ref{section:TWAP}. The goal of this section is to emphasize the performance of the baseline DQN-learned policy compared with the continuous-control extended policies and the benchmarks. We start by generating an emulator of the CLOB followed by the closing auction. The algorithm used to generate the market mechanism is defined in Section \ref{sub:generative} with Algorithm \ref{alg:generative_model}. In Section \ref{sub:rough} we generate mid price process values by using the rough-Heston to describe the evolution of the price of a risky asset, see \cite{gatheral2022volatility}. Finally, in Section \ref{sub:historical}
we train and test our DQN-learned policy to find the optimal trading strategy along a trading day with historical data for CAT, PG, GOOGL, JPM and MSFT. This second simulation is referred to as ``historical'' for simplicity. In both cases (generated or historical mid-price input) we note that our learning algorithm outperform the benchmarks on the mean returns.\vspace{0.5em}

In both simulations we consider the following setting: a two-hour CLOB phase (120 nominal one-minute steps) followed by a 30-minute closing auction (30 steps), with the mid price generated by the rough Heston model presented in Section \ref{subsub:roughheston} or with historical mid-price input. This corresponds roughly to what happens at exchanges like Xetra and the CLOB phase can be viewed as the trading sessions leading up to the closing auction. We train the models on 1{,}000 (resp. 500) episodes in the synthetic (resp. ``historical'') simulations and then run an additional 100 episodes keeping the policy fixed for evaluation. The networks receive normalized versions of physical time, decision index and continuous features, with normalization fitted on training data only. Throughout training, we consider a validation set which allows to select the report policy by early stopping.

\subsection{Generative Stochastic Market Model}
\label{sub:generative}

We now explain in details our market emulator to generate the financial market in our setting. 
The algorithm is described in Algorithm \ref{alg:generative_model}. It specifies only the exogenous random variables and accepted exogenous book updates. Let us precise that we say that a candidate exogenous linear auction book is valid if the aggregate slope $G_t \geqslant D_\mu$ for a numerical lower slope bound $D_\mu >0$ and there is a nonnegative continuous clearing root, as in Proposition \ref{prop:linear}.\vspace{0.5em} 

\begin{algorithm}[ht!]
\caption{Exogenous stochastic market generator}
\label{alg:generative_model}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Parameter vector
$(\lambda_0,v_m,\gamma_m,V,\beta_a,\beta_b,V_\infty,
\rho,U_1,U_2,M_1,M_2,p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)$
\STATE Sample the independent CLOB counting processes
$N^+,N^-\sim\mathrm{PP}(\lambda_0)$ on $[0,\tau^\op]$

\STATE Construct the complete realized grid
$(t_i)_{0\leqslant  i\leqslant  m+1}$ according to
Definition~\ref{def:timing}

\FOR{$k=0,\ldots,n$}
    \FOR{$\zeta\in\{+,-\}$}
        \STATE Sample $V_{t_k}^{\zeta,1}
        \sim
        V_\infty\operatorname{Beta}(\beta_a,\beta_b)$
\FOR{$\ell=1,\ldots,L_{\mathrm{max}}-1$}
            \STATE Set $V_{t_k}^{\zeta,\ell+1}
            =
            \rho V_{t_k}^{\zeta,\ell}$
        \ENDFOR

        \FOR{$r=N_{t_k}^{\zeta}+1,\ldots,
                   N_{t_{k+1}}^{\zeta}$}
            \STATE Sample $Z_{k,r}^{\zeta}
            \sim
            \operatorname{Pareto}(v_m,\gamma_m)$ and set $\nu_{k,r}^{\zeta}
            =
            \min\{Z_{k,r}^{\zeta},V\}$
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR

    \STATE Send exogenous CLOB snapshot and sampled arrivals on $(t_k,t_{k+1}]$ to the controlled env
\ENDFOR

\STATE Receive from the controlled environment the post-execution
residual exogenous CLOB multiset $O_{\tau^\op-}^{\star}$, recompute the final Algorithm \ref{alg:hyp_clearing_price} calibration, and initialize the carry-over schedules calibration from this residual multiset and initialize the carry-over schedules \[\mathcal B_{\tau^\op}^{\star}
=
\left\{
g_{k,\tau^\op}^{\star}:
k\in\mathcal K_{\star}
\right\}\]

\STATE Initialize the exogenous auction book from the carry-over book
and set $H_{t_{n+1}}^\cl\gets H_{t_n}^\cl$
\FOR{$k=n+1,\ldots,m$}
    \STATE Sample proposed auction updates $B_{t_k}\sim\mathcal B(p_1)$, $D_{t_k}\sim\mathcal B(p_2)$, $J_{t_k}^{\pm}\sim\mathcal B(p_3)$, $G_{t_k}^{\pm}\sim\mathcal B(p_4)$

    \IF{$B_{t_k}=1$}
        \STATE Sample $K_{t_k}^r\sim\mathcal U([U_1,U_2])$, $S_{t_k}^r
        \sim
        S_{\tau^\op}^{\mathrm{mid}}
        +
        \alpha\mathcal U(\llbracket M_1,M_2\rrbracket)$
        and add the new exogenous schedule
    \ENDIF

    \IF{$D_{t_k}=1$ and a nonpersistent exogenous schedule is present}
        \STATE Propose canceling one uniformly selected exogenous schedule and accept the cancellation only if the resulting exogenous book remains valid
    \ENDIF

    \FOR{$\zeta\in\{+,-\}$}
        \IF{$J_{t_k}^{\zeta}=1$}
            \STATE Sample $Z_{t_k,\zeta}
            \sim
            \operatorname{Pareto}(v_m,\gamma_m)$ and set $\nu_{t_k}^{\zeta}
            =
            \min\{Z_{t_k,\zeta},V\}$
            \STATE Add the corresponding market order if the resulting book is valid
        \ENDIF

        \IF{$G_{t_k}^{\zeta}=1$ and an active market order is present
        on side $\zeta$}
            \STATE Cancel a uniformly sampled order if the resulting book is valid
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \IF{$k=m$}
        \STATE Process no further
        exogenous update
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

At each auction decision time, the accepted exogenous update returned by Algorithm \ref{alg:generative_model} is incorporated into the current configuration before the agent acts via the following chronology.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $k=n+1$, set
    $H_{t_{n+1}}^\cl=H_{t_n}^\cl$. If $k\geqslant n+2$, compute
    $H_{t_k}^\cl$ from the complete post-action book stored at
    $t_{k-1}$.
    \item Sample the exogenous proposals at $t_k$ and accept each
    proposal only if the resulting exogenous book remains valid.
    \item Construct the pre-action configuration $X_{t_k}$ and reveal
    $\varphi(X_{t_k})$ to the agent.
    \item If $c_{t_k}=1$, remove every still-active agent schedule
    submitted strictly before $t_k$.
    \item Add the current schedule $p\longmapsto
    K_{t_k}^a
    \left(
    p-S_{\tau^\op}^{\mathrm{mid}}-\alpha b_{t_k}^a
    \right).$
    \item Store the resulting post-action book. If $k=m$, freeze this
    book, process no event on $(t_m,\tau^\cl]$, and apply the terminal
    clearing and allocation rule.
\end{enumerate}
After the final action at $t_m$, the controlled environment freezes the post-action book and applies the clearing and allocation rule of Section \ref{sec:clearing}.\vspace{0.5em}

The inputs of our emulator are given by
$ (\lambda_0,v_m,\gamma_m,V, \beta_a,\beta_b,V_\infty, \rho, U_1,U_2,M_1,M_2, p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\lambda_0$ represents the intensity of the counting processes $N^+$ and $N^-$ on the continuous phase, modeled as independent Poisson processes, that are the arrivals per side per minute. The two processes are not observable for the agent during the continuous phase, but only during the auction phase. They are sampled in the first step of the algorithm.
    \item $(v_m,\gamma_m)$ are the parameters of the Pareto distribution modeling the volumes of the limit orders throughout the whole trading sessions. This reproduces the well-known heavy tail behavior of the density of market order size, see for example \cite{gopikrishnan2000statistical,gabaix2009power,bouchaud2001power}. The market model allows orders of maximum size $V$: greater liquidity takers will accumulate at volume $V$.
    \item $(\beta_a,\beta_b)$ represent the parameters of the Beta distribution modeling the first volume of the limit order book. It is similar to the Beta scaling effect described in for example \cite{jerome2022market,wang2024market}.  Note that $V_\infty$ is a rescaling parameter as the Beta distribution has support $[0,1]$. Further volumes in the limit order book decay geometrically with parameter $\rho \in (0,1]$ and are truncated via the maximum order book depth $L_\infty$, that is $V_t^{\zeta,j}=0$ for $j > L_\infty$.
    \item $(U_1,U_2)$ represent the bounds between which exogenous market maker sample their supply slopes from during the auction phase.
    \item $(M_1,M_2)$ represent the integer bounds of the price at which exogenous market makers quote during the auction phase. Exogenous market makers during the volume are assumed to be sampled as $S_t^i \sim S_{\tau^\op}^\mathrm{mid} + \alpha \mathcal{U}(\llbracket M_1,M_2 \rrbracket)$ because the auction opening time is the last time market participants see the mid price. Note that this shape of auction price have been introduced in \cite{derchu2023equilibria}. 
    \item $(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)$ represent the independent proposal probabilities per one-minute auction decision that drive the market structure during the auction phase. A new market maker arrives per step with proposal probability $p_1$, one market maker cancels his order with proposal probability $p_2$, a market taker arrives on either side with proposal probability $p_3$, and a market taker cancels his order with proposal probability $p_4$, all the conditioned on the book being still valid. These events are sampled using a Bernoulli distribution.
\end{itemize}

The proposal variables and their marks are sampled independently. The accepted book updates are state-dependent because of the validity screen. We do assume that the limit order book is refreshed from one time step to the next one. Lines 3 to 6 describe the mechanics of the continuous phase. Lines 7 to 24 describe the mechanics of the auction phase. Finally, we precise that all sampled quantities are projected on the grid we fixed in Section \ref{sec:MDP}.\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Rough Heston Model for the Mid Price: Generative Data Approach}
\label{sub:rough}
In this section, we generate data from the rough-Heston model.

\subsubsection{Numerical Method and Parameter Calibration}
\label{subsub:roughheston}

In the first numerical implementation, we assume that the mid price $S_t$ follows a rough Heston model. The motivation is based on the so-called rough volatility of financial assets \cite{gatheral2022volatility,bäuerle2020portfolio,abi2019multifactor}. Consider $\rho \in [-1,1]$ a constant, $S_0 = 100$ (which is the numerical value we work with in this section), $V_0$, $H$, $\theta$, $\varsigma$ and $\nu$ be positive constants. Recalling \cite{gatheral2022volatility,richard2023discrete}, the rough Heston model writes
\[\left\{\begin{array}{l}
    S_t = S_0 + \int_0^t S_s \sqrt{V_s}\mathrm{d}\big( \rho B_s + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}B_s^\perp\big), \\
    V_t = V_0 + \int_0^t K_H(t-s)\big ((\theta-\varsigma V_s)\mathrm{d}s + \nu \sqrt{V_s}\mathrm{d}B_s \big),
\end{array}\right.\]
where $(B,B^\perp)$ are two independent Brownian motions and $K_H(t) = t^{H-1/2}/\Gamma(H+1/2)$ for $t>0$. \vspace{0.5em}

We used the Euler-type scheme of \cite{richard2023discrete} to approximate this rough Heston model. For each episode, we first construct the realized CLOB grid $0=t_0<\cdots<t_n=\tau^\op-1<t_{n+1}=\tau^\op.$ Conditional on this complete grid, and independently of the order-flow processes, we simulate the rough-Heston approximation on the resulting
nonuniform mesh. We use the model time measured in trading years. Thus, for $s^\star = 252\cdot 6.5\cdot 60$, let $\bar{t}_i = t_i/s^\star$. We set $\Delta \bar{t}_{i+1}^n=\bar{t}_{i+1}-\bar{t}_i$ for $i\in \llbracket 0,n \rrbracket$ and generate the Brownian increments with variance $\Delta \bar{t}_{i+1}^n$. The midprice is simulated through $t_{n+1}=\tau^\op$, projected to the tick grid, and then frozen throughout the auction. Then $S_{k} = \exp(Y_k)$ for $k \in \llbracket 0,n+1 \rrbracket$ where
\[\left\{\begin{array}{l}
    Y_k =  \displaystyle Y_0 + \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \left( -\frac{1}{2} (V_i)_+ \Delta \bar{t}_{i+1}^n + \rho \sqrt{(V_i)_+}\left(B_{\bar{t}_{i+1}}-B_{\bar{t}_i}\right) + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\sqrt{(V_i)_+}\left(B_{\bar{t}_{i+1}}^\perp-B_{\bar{t}_i}^\perp\right)\right), \\
    V_k =  \displaystyle V_0 + \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \left( K_H(\bar{t}_k - \bar{t}_i)(\theta - \varsigma (V_i)_+)\Delta \bar{t}_{i+1}^n + K_H(\bar{t}_k - \bar{t}_i)\nu\sqrt{(V_i)_+}\left(B_{\bar{t}_{i+1}}-B_{\bar{t}_i}\right) \right ).\end{array}\right.
\]
At time $t_k$, the midprice revealed to the trading environment is $S_{t_k}^{\mathrm{mid}} = \alpha \lfloor S_k/\alpha+1/2\rfloor$. In numerical applications, we used the illustrative parameters $H=0.1$, $\rho = -0.7$, $V_0 = 0.02$, $\theta=0.02$, $\varsigma = 0.3$ and $\nu = 0.3$ adapted from \cite{abi2019lifting}.\vspace{0.5em}

Now, one may notice that the AS stylized reference benchmark assumes that $\mathrm{d}S_t = \sigma \mathrm{d}B_t$, instead of the rough Heston model. In this sense, the goal of the numerical simulation is to compare how our deep reinforcement learning policies performs against a stylized reference benchmark, without claiming optimality of this benchmark. The benchmark is deliberately stylized and is not optimal for the simulated rough-Heston environment; it is therefore interpreted as a reference policy rather than as an oracle comparator.\vspace{0.5em}

We calibrated the value $\sigma$ by estimating the standard deviation of the mid price on the trading period. From \cite{avellaneda2008high}, we have $A = \lambda_0/\gamma_m$ and $k = \alpha K$. Note that $A$ is per minute and the estimated volatility $\sigma$ is per square-root minute. Here, $K$ is the proportionality constant in the empirical relationship $K\Delta p = \ln(Q)$, where $\Delta p$ is the move in price when a market order of size $Q$ arrives. We did a least squares regression to determine $K$, by simulating 5{,}000 samples of the limit order book and market orders $Q \sim \mathrm{Pareto}(v_m,\gamma_m)$.

\section{Conclusion}

We formulate a two-phase liquidation problem that combines one-sided CLOB execution with signed participation in a stylized closing call, and develop feature-based reinforcement-learning methods for the resulting controlled simulator. Our findings suggest that RL has the potential to be effective for market making in complex structures beyond simple LOBs with closing auction, as for example workup session or AHEAD mechanism \cite{duffie2017size,derchu2024ahead} or sequence of periodic auctions \cite{milgrom1985economics,madhavan1992trading, budish2014implementation}.\vspace{0.5em}

The model is a single-agent best-response problem against fixed stochastic background policies; it does not model strategic responses or market equilibrium. The auction chronology is sequential and includes a final reaction opportunity for the agent. The background CLOB is refreshed between decisions, and the carry-over is a calibrated schedule representation rather than a literal order transfer. The reduced feature process need not be Markov. Negative terminal inventory is permitted, but post-close securities-borrow and financing costs are not modeled explicitly; terminal exposure is instead marked to the frozen midprice and penalized quadratically. Historical-input experiments retain synthetic order flow and auction dynamics.
